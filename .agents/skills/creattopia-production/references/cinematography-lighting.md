# Cinematography Rules and On-Camera Execution

Applies to `th` (talking head) and `hv` (handheld vlog) shots. Note: some of the source's numeric specifics (exact grid dimensions, lighting ratios) weren't preserved in the extracted text — where that happens below, standard filmmaking convention is used and flagged as such rather than presented as a verbatim Creattopia spec.

## Solo framing mechanics

A solo creator has no camera operator, so visual variety has to come from the creator's own physical movement and repositioning rather than a second person adjusting the shot:

- Move toward/away from the lens between beats instead of staying static.
- Re-rack framing (reframe distance/angle) between takes rather than holding one setup for an entire video.
- Use foreground props (objects, doorframes, furniture) to create layered depth even with a single camera.

## Shot distances

Vary shot distance deliberately across a script rather than defaulting to one medium shot for the whole video — mix wide/establishing, medium (chest-up), and close-up (face-focused) shots to control pacing and emotional emphasis. Pair distance choices with the visual-asset refresh cadence in `editing-post-production.md`.

## Camera angle psychology

Camera height/angle changes how the viewer perceives the subject (standard convention, not exhaustively itemized in the source):

- **Low angle** (camera below eye line, looking up) — reads as powerful, dominant, or larger-than-life.
- **Eye-level** — reads as neutral, relatable, trustworthy; the default for most talking-head content.
- **High angle** (camera above eye line, looking down) — reads as vulnerable, small, or subordinate.

Pick the angle deliberately per the emotional beat of that line, not by camera-placement convenience.

## Grid alignment ("powerpoints")

Align key elements — eyes, faces, important props — with the grid lines or their intersections (referred to in the source as **powerpoints**), consistent with standard rule-of-thirds composition. Background elements (walls, doorframes, desks, window blinds) should also line up with grid lines to keep the frame visually level and clean. Cameras with a built-in on-screen grid (e.g. Sony bodies) make this alignment mechanical — use the physical grid overlay rather than eyeballing it.

## Lighting design

Proper lighting is what separates a "professional, polished" look from a flat or muddy one. The source doesn't specify an exact lighting ratio/setup, so apply standard practice unless the creator has a specific rig already:

- Key light as the dominant source, angled to avoid flattening the face.
- Fill (softer, dimmer) to control shadow contrast rather than eliminate it entirely.
- Keep color temperature consistent across all sources in frame to avoid mixed-white-balance footage that reads as amateur.
