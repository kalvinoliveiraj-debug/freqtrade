# Advanced Editing Workflows and Post-Production

A structured, repeatable post-production pipeline is what converts raw footage into a high-retention edit. Where the source's specific bullet lists weren't preserved in extraction, this file states the technique and gives standard implementation practice, flagged as such.

## Multi-timeline editing and dynamic pacing

Structure the edit as **layered tracks**, not a single flat sequence:

- A dedicated track/layer for primary on-camera footage.
- A separate track/layer for B-roll and cutaway visual assets.
- A separate track/layer for text/graphics overlays.
- A separate track/layer for music and a separate one for SFX.

Keeping footage grouped this way lets you apply color correction or an effect to the entire primary-footage group at once, so the whole edit reads as visually consistent instead of shot-by-shot inconsistent.

## Visual asset refresh cadence

Retention drops when the same visual sits on screen too long without a purpose-driven cut. The rule: change the visual asset — via composition, distance, angle, focal length, or camera movement — on a strict, frequent cadence throughout the edit (the source specifies "every few seconds" without a hard number; tune this per genre — Mr. Nightmare-style slow-burn content deliberately violates this rule for effect, see `faceless-channels.md`).

## J-cuts

Let the incoming clip's **audio** start a fraction of a second before its **visual** appears, while the outgoing clip's visual is still on screen. This overlap smooths the transition and hides the cut point, which keeps pacing feeling continuous rather than choppy.

## Pattern breaks

Deliberately disrupt the established visual/audio rhythm to re-engage a viewer whose attention may be drifting. Options:

- Cut the music entirely for up to ~3 seconds.
- Insert a subtle zoom (push-in/push-out) that isn't part of the established cutting rhythm.
- Cut to a comedic close-up paired with a surprising sound effect.

Use pattern breaks sparingly — their power comes from violating an expectation the edit has already established, so overusing them removes the effect.

## Compound time-lapse skit technique

Shows a sequence of actions (e.g. a multi-day challenge, a build, a transformation) inside a single fast-paced clip instead of a long real-time sequence:

1. Shoot each discrete action/beat as its own short clip, keeping camera position and framing consistent across beats so the cuts read as one continuous space.
2. Cut the beats together back-to-back with no dead time between them, letting the natural jump between setups imply the passage of time.
3. Carry one continuous audio bed (music or VO) across all the beats so the sequence reads as one edited unit rather than a slideshow.
4. Reserve a final beat for the payoff/result, mirroring the payoff structure used in Surreal E-Commerce ads (see `surreal-ecommerce.md`).

## DIY skateboard dolly

A budget way to get smooth, cinematic tracking shots without a dedicated dolly rig:

1. Mount the camera (on a small tripod or rig plate) securely to a skateboard deck.
2. Use a smooth, flat, obstacle-free surface for the push.
3. Have a second person push the skateboard at a slow, even pace while the operator handles focus/framing, or rig a pull-cord for a solo operator.
4. Shoot multiple passes at slightly different speeds and pick the smoothest take in the edit rather than trying to nail it live.

## Kinetic typography and font design rules

Text overlays/captions must stay readable and support the narrative rather than compete with it:

- Keep a consistent font family and weight across a whole video/series so text reads as a system, not ad hoc decoration.
- Ensure sufficient contrast between text and background (stroke/shadow/background plate if the underlying footage is busy).
- Time text animation to land on the beat of the word being spoken, not just appear/disappear arbitrarily.
- Keep on-screen text short — captions support comprehension and retention, they aren't a transcript.
